# Profile, uniqueness and market placement

<!--
  Why this file exists: a verdict and a competency table say whether someone
  meets a bar. They do not say who the person is. Hiring managers and
  designers both want the second answer: what makes this profile distinct,
  where it fits, where it would struggle. This is also where reviews most
  easily turn into flattery, so the evidence rules apply in full.
-->

## What this section answers

1. **What this portfolio says about the person**, in three to five plain
   sentences, as a hiring manager would describe them to a colleague after
   reading it. Not a summary of the case studies; a read of the person.
2. **Profile shape**: which competencies are strongest and weakest relative
   to each other, and what that combination usually means in practice.
3. **Distinctive signals**: what this portfolio shows that most portfolios at
   this level do not.
4. **Market placement**: what kinds of roles and companies this profile
   fits, and where it would be a weak fit.

## Distinctive signals

A distinctive signal must meet all three conditions:

- It is backed by Demonstrated or Specific evidence, cited.
- It recurs across more than one case study, or is unusually strong in one.
- It is uncommon at this level. Explain why it is uncommon in one line,
  using reasoning, not statistics.

Examples of the kind of thing that qualifies: a consistent pattern of
reporting outcomes against baselines; working prototypes in code used to
make decisions; deep domain experience in a regulated or technical field;
leading distributed teams across very different markets; designing failure
and uncertainty states for model-based products; a design system documented
precisely enough for tooling to consume.

Things that do not qualify on their own: years of experience, well-known
employer names, a strong tagline, visual polish alone, tool lists.

If nothing qualifies, say so. "No clear differentiator" is a valid and useful
finding, especially in self-assessment mode.

## Profile shape

Describe the shape, do not average it:

- Name the strongest two or three competencies and the weakest one or two.
- Use the wheel's neighbour structure where it helps: strengths in adjacent
  competencies usually reinforce each other (for example Building and UX &
  Research suggest someone who learns by making). Placement on the wheel has
  no other meaning.
- Say what the shape suggests the person would be best and worst at in a
  team, phrased as what the portfolio presents.

## Market placement

<!--

  the context notes when hiring patterns shift. Keep claims qualitative.
-->

Market context as of late 2026, for reasoning only:

- AI has made production cheap. Hiring managers weight judgment, selection,
  evidence of outcomes and ownership more than artifact volume.
- Demand is strong for product designers who build (code prototypes, AI
  coding tools) and for those who can design model-based products. Pure
  visual or handoff-only profiles face more competition.
- Many companies expect fewer, more senior designers with wider scope.
  Middle-management design roles are fewer and more often player-coach.
- Titles vary widely between companies, so scope evidence matters more than
  titles.

Placement output:

- **Best fit**: company stage, product type, team shape and role type this
  portfolio supports. Be specific ("an early AI product team that needs a
  designer who prototypes in code" rather than "tech companies").
- **Weak fit**: where the same profile would struggle, and why.
- **Competitive position**: how the portfolio would likely compare against
  others applying to the target role, in qualitative terms (stands out on X,
  ordinary on Y, behind on Z). Never use invented percentiles or rankings.
- If a JD was supplied, map its requirements to the evidence explicitly.

If web search is available and the user asked for market context, current job
postings for the target role can be used to check what is being asked for.
Cite them. Do not search by default.
