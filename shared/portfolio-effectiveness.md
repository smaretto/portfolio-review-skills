# Portfolio effectiveness

<!--
  Why this is a separate layer: competencies describe the designer; this file
  describes the portfolio as a piece of communication. A strong designer with
  a weak portfolio and a weak designer with a slick one must not get blurred
  together. The competency ratings say what the evidence shows; this section
  says how well the portfolio gets that evidence across to a busy reader.
-->

## The reader

Assume the first reader is a hiring manager or recruiter with a few minutes
per portfolio, skimming before deciding whether to read properly. Most
portfolios are judged on the home page, the first case study's opening and
the images, before any detailed reading. Assess both passes:

- **Skim pass (first 2 minutes):** what does the reader conclude about who
  this is, what level, what niche, from headlines, intro, case study titles
  and images only?
- **Deep pass:** does the detailed reading confirm, sharpen or contradict the
  skim impression?

A large gap between the two passes is itself a finding. The most common one:
strong work buried under a generic intro and process-heavy openings.

## Dimensions

Rate each as **Strong**, **Adequate** or **Weak**, with the evidence.

### 1. Positioning and niche

- Can a reader state in one sentence what this designer is for? (Domain,
  product type, problem type, or way of working.)
- Is the niche backed by the case studies, or only by the intro text?
- Does the positioning match the target role? A designer positioning as
  "AI-first design engineer" applying to a brand-heavy consumer role has a
  positioning problem even if the work is good.
- "Generalist" is a valid position if stated as such and evidenced by range.
  "I do everything" without depth in anything is not.

### 2. Case selection

- Number of case studies: three to five deep ones usually beat ten shallow
  ones. Note if there are too few to judge or too many to read.
- Recency: is recent work present and first?
- Range versus focus: does the selection support the positioning?
- Level fit: do the chosen projects show work at the target level, or the
  candidate's best IC work when applying for a leadership role (or the
  reverse)?

### 3. Storytelling

- Does each case study open with the problem and why it mattered, not with
  the solution or a process diagram?
- Is the candidate's role and the team context stated early?
- Is there a clear arc: context, problem, decisions, trade-offs, outcome?
- Are decisions visible, or only activities ("we did research, then
  wireframes, then UI")? A process list is not a story.
- Is the outcome stated, measured where possible, and honest about misses?
- Length: does each case study earn its length? Flag walls of text and flag
  image dumps with no narrative.

### 4. Visual evidence and craft presentation

<!--
  Only assess from page images. If none were seen, rate as "Not assessed".
-->

- Are final, high-quality assets shown, not only process artifacts?
- Is process shown where it matters (rejected options, iterations), not as
  decoration (sticky-note photos, generic journey maps)?
- Does the presentation itself show craft: hierarchy, type, consistency,
  image quality, cropping?
- Are states, edge cases and responsive sizes shown, or only hero screens?
- Motion and video: present or absent, where placed, what they claim to show.
  For work where behaviour matters (interactions, AI states, prototypes),
  absence of any moving or interactive evidence is worth noting. Do not judge
  the quality of motion that was not watched.
- Does the visual weight suit the role? A product role needs flows and
  decisions; a craft-led role needs high-finish assets up front.

### 5. Readability and scannability

- Headings, summaries and captions that let a skimmer get the point.
- A short summary per case study (role, team, duration, outcome) near the
  top.
- Navigation: can the reader find the work in one click?
- Hidden narrative: is the core story (context, role, scope, outcome) visible
  on the page, or behind accordions, tabs or "read more" clicks? Readers who
  skim rarely open them, so evidence hidden there is evidence most readers
  never see.
- Writing quality: clear, concise, free of jargon and filler.

### 6. Professional hygiene

- Working links, readable images, no lorem ipsum, no broken embeds.
- Confidentiality handled sensibly (NDA work described or anonymised, not
  just omitted or recklessly exposed).
- Contact information and CV available.
- Numbers consistent across pages.
- Machine readability: hiring teams increasingly screen with AI tools,
  which read text and alt text, not pixels. Generic or missing alt text
  ("Placeholder image", "image1") makes the visual evidence invisible or
  misread, and text set inside images is not read at all. Descriptive alt
  text and key facts in live text fix both.

## Output

In the report, give each dimension its rating and one or two lines of
evidence. Then name the **single change that would most improve how the
portfolio reads**. That change is often more valuable to the user than the
full list.
