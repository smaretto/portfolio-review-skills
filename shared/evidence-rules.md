# Evidence rules

<!--
  Why this file exists: a portfolio is a document written by the candidate to
  make the candidate look good. A reviewer that takes its claims at face value
  produces flattering nonsense. These rules decide how much weight each claim
  carries before any competency is rated.
-->

## Evidence strength

Classify every piece of evidence you use into one of four tiers. The tier
matters more than how impressive the claim sounds.

| Tier | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Demonstrated** | The artifact itself shows it | Rejected options shown next to the chosen one with reasons; a working prototype link; a metric chart with baseline and date range |
| **Specific** | Described with checkable detail: method, numbers, own role, dates | "Ran 12 moderated tests with returning customers; 3 of 5 tasks failed on the date picker, so we moved it" |
| **Asserted** | Stated without support | "Improved conversion significantly", "led the strategy" |
| **Absent** | Not in the portfolio | |

Rules:

- A rating of At level or Above for a competency needs at least one
  Demonstrated or Specific piece of evidence for it. Assertions alone cap the
  rating at Below, however confident the wording.
- Several assertions do not add up to one Specific piece of evidence.
- Quote or closely paraphrase the evidence and say where it is (case study
  name, page number). The reader must be able to check it.

## Attribution

Separate what the candidate did from what happened around them.

- **Company outcomes are not personal outcomes.** Funding raised, company
  growth, an acquisition or an award belong to the company unless the
  portfolio shows a causal line from the candidate's work to the result.
  "Joined before the Series B" is context, not achievement.
- **"We" needs an "I".** In team projects, credit only what the candidate
  says they personally did or decided. If a case study uses "we" throughout,
  record that the individual contribution is unclear.
- **Metrics need a baseline and a scope.** "+2.3% conversion" without a
  baseline, a time frame, a test design or the candidate's role in it is
  Asserted, not Specific. Treat round, large numbers with more scepticism,
  not less.
- **Inconsistent figures.** When the same figure differs between pages (team
  size, number of reports, dates), use the more conservative one and note
  the discrepancy. Repeated inconsistencies are a Watch red flag.
- **Scope claims need structure.** "Led a 100-person org" means nothing
  without reporting lines. Prefer the most conservative reading the text
  supports, and state the reading you took.

## Slogans and positioning lines

Taglines ("I build teams that don't wait for a brief") are positioning, not
evidence. Assess whether the case studies back them up. Never infer
personality traits or working style from a slogan alone.

## Claims about AI

- Using AI tools in the process is not the same as designing AI products.
  Keep them apart (see the AI as Material competency).
- Volume of AI-assisted output is not evidence of seniority. Selection,
  judgment and outcomes are.
- "AI-powered" or "AI-native" as a label, without design consequences shown,
  is Asserted.

## Absence

Two kinds of absence, recorded differently:

- **Not shown**: the competency would be expected in the work presented and
  there is no evidence of it. This counts against the candidate.
- **No opportunity**: the work presented gives no reason to expect it (for
  example AI as Material when none of the products use models). This is
  neutral unless the target role or JD requires it, in which case it is a gap
  for that role, stated as such.

A portfolio is a curated sample. Gaps describe what the portfolio presents,
not proven limits of the person. Phrase them that way ("the portfolio does not
show...") and list the important ones as questions for an interview.

## Inference limits

The review may derive what kind of designer the portfolio presents: level,
archetype, strengths, likely gaps. It may not:

- infer personality, motivation or culture fit beyond what the work shows
- invent market statistics or percentiles ("top-decile", "top 5%")
- assume work exists that is not shown
- use age, gender, nationality, name or any personal characteristic in the
  assessment
