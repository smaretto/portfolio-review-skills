## 1. The wheel

Four headings, each with a core competency, and four bridging competencies
between them. Clockwise from the top:

| Position | Competency |
| --- | --- |
| Make | Craft & Taste |
| Make / Understand | Building |
| Understand | UX & Research |
| Understand / Decide | AI as Material |
| Decide | Product Judgment |
| Decide / Drive | Influence |
| Drive | Ownership |
| Drive / Make | Systems Thinking |

Neighbouring competencies tend to appear together. Use this when describing
the profile shape. Do not read meaning into opposite positions.

## 2. Assessment principles

- **Observable evidence, not adjectives.** "Tested key interactions in a
  coded prototype with real data before handoff" is assessable. "Strong
  technical skills" is not.
- **AI tool fluency is not a competency.** Assess its effect inside the
  others: faster synthesis shows up in UX & Research, more explored
  directions in Craft & Taste. AI as Material (designing products that use
  models) is a separate, lasting competency.
- **Intent is not evidence.** Interest or appetite counts for little.
- **Ownership needs its counterweight.** Reward outcomes and the judgment to
  stop, not shipping volume.
- **Re-anchor to current practice.** What counted as advanced prototyping a
  few years ago is baseline now. Old anchors inflate ratings.
- **Separate the individual from the team.**
- **Read the shape, not only the ratings.**

Level columns below: Junior, Mid, Senior, Staff, Principal. For Senior Staff,
expect Staff evidence at a wider radius, or Principal evidence in one area.
See levels.md for the level definitions and US tech mapping.

## 3. Craft & Taste (Make)

The quality of what the designer makes and, above all, of what they choose.
When generating options is free, taste is the differentiator: editing,
selecting, and explaining why one option beats another.

Look for: alternatives shown with the reason others were rejected; rationale
tied to users, content or constraints; consistent finish across states, edge
cases and sizes.
Weak signals: polished screens with no visible selection; trend-following
style regardless of audience; rationale limited to adjectives ("clean",
"modern").

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Junior | Clean, consistent execution within an existing system. Can explain basic choices (hierarchy, spacing). |
| Mid | Solid finish across states and sizes. Shows some alternatives and picks with a stated reason. |
| Senior | Consistent high finish including edge cases. Alternatives and rejected directions shown with user or business reasoning. Visual and interaction choices fit the product, not trends. |
| Staff | Sets or raises the quality bar on a product area. Craft decisions visible across multiple surfaces; others' work improved by their standards. |
| Principal | Defines the quality bar or design language for a product or company. Choices shape how the product feels across teams. |

Reviewer caution: this is where an AI reviewer is least reliable. Assess
what is checkable (consistency, hierarchy, shown alternatives, stated
rationale). Do not claim to judge taste. Mark confidence low if page images
were not seen.

## 4. Building (Make / Understand)

Making working things: coded prototypes, AI-assisted code, small production
contributions, tests in real conditions with real data. A coded prototype is
both a made thing and a way to learn whether an idea works.

Look for: working prototypes (links, repos, videos) used to test or decide
something; an account of what the prototype revealed and what changed;
interactions only credible in code (latency, real content, data states).
Weak signals: "technical appetite" with nothing built; prototypes for show;
click-through mockups called prototypes of complex behaviour.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Junior | Interactive prototypes in design tools; some exposure to AI prototyping tools. |
| Mid | Prototypes beyond click-through for key interactions; some AI-assisted code prototypes. |
| Senior | Uses coded or AI-built prototypes to test ideas with real content or data, and the result changed a decision. Works with engineers at implementation level. |
| Staff | Building is a regular decision tool on their area; may contribute production code or tooling; helps others build. |
| Principal | Shapes how the team prototypes and builds (tooling, workflows, design and code integration). |

Archetype note: for Builder / design engineer profiles, expect Above here and
check that building serves decisions, not just output.

## 5. UX & Research (Understand)

Understanding users, context and problems, and validating with real
evidence. Primary user contact matters more because building the wrong thing
is now fast and cheap. Synthetic users and AI summaries do not count as
evidence on their own.

Look for: research the designer ran or shaped, with method, participants and
learnings; a traceable line from finding to design change; testing before and
after shipping, including results that contradicted assumptions.
Weak signals: "users told us" with no method; personas or journeys with no
effect on the product; synthetic users as primary evidence.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Junior | Takes part in research; can state what was learned and how it shaped their screens. |
| Mid | Runs usability tests independently; clear flows and IA; findings visibly change designs. |
| Senior | Plans research for their area, chooses methods with reasons, tests before and after launch, reports results that contradicted the team. |
| Staff | Shapes research strategy for a product area; connects research across teams; findings change roadmaps, not only screens. |
| Principal | Shapes how the organisation learns from users; research changes product direction. |

## 6. AI as Material (Understand / Decide)

Designing products that use models. Distinct from using AI tools to design.
Covers non-deterministic output, failure and uncertainty states, trust,
appropriate reliance, and defining what good output means.

Look for: failure, low-confidence and edge-case states, not only the ideal
response; evaluation criteria for model output; decisions about where AI
should not be used.
Weak signals: AI features shown only in the happy path; confusing AI products
with AI tools; "AI-powered" as a label with no design consequences.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Junior | Designs AI feature screens including some error states, with guidance. |
| Mid | Designs full AI flows including failure, loading and correction states. |
| Senior | Defines quality criteria for model output on their feature; designs for trust and appropriate reliance; decides where not to use AI. |
| Staff | Sets AI interaction patterns across a product area; works with ML or eng on evaluation. |
| Principal | Shapes the company's approach to AI product experience. |

Rate **No opportunity** when no product shown uses models, unless the target
role or JD requires it.

## 7. Product Judgment (Decide)

Deciding what to build and why: problem framing, prioritisation, success
definition, trade-offs against business, user and impact goals. Covers what
used to be split into strategic thinking and business acumen.

Look for: a clearly framed problem and why it mattered to users and the
business; success metrics defined up front and results reported against them,
including misses; explicit trade-offs (cut, deferred, not built, and why).
Weak signals: case studies that start with the solution; outcomes without
baseline; every decision presented as obviously right.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Junior | Understands and restates the problem and goal they were given. |
| Mid | Frames feature-level problems; knows the success metric; makes scoped trade-offs. |
| Senior | Frames problems for their area, defines success up front, reports results honestly, makes and explains real trade-offs with PM. |
| Staff | Shapes what gets built across a product area; connects design decisions to the business model; influences prioritisation. |
| Principal | Shapes product strategy; decisions affect multiple products or the company's direction. |

## 8. Influence (Decide / Drive)

Changing what others decide or do: presenting and defending rationale,
running critique, writing decision documents, aligning partners, raising
peers' quality through feedback.

Look for: situations where the designer changed a decision, roadmap or
practice, and how; documents, critique formats or principles others adopted;
honest accounts of disagreement.
Weak signals: "worked closely with PM and engineering" as the whole story;
outcomes credited to the designer that depended on others; no visible role in
large teams.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Junior | Presents own work clearly; takes feedback. |
| Mid | Defends decisions with reasons; collaborates well with PM and eng on their feature. |
| Senior | Changes decisions within the team; gives useful critique; aligns cross-functional partners on their area. |
| Staff | Changes roadmaps or practices beyond their team; artifacts adopted by others; mentors seniors. |
| Principal | Changes how the design org or product org works; sets direction others follow. |

## 9. Ownership (Drive)

Bias for action: acting as one's own PM, deciding under uncertainty, driving
work to shipped outcomes, pulling in the people needed. Includes knowing when
not to build and stopping work based on evidence. Ownership is moving work
forward oneself; Influence is changing what others do.

Look for: work the designer initiated or pushed through, with what they
personally did; shipped outcomes and what happened after launch; work stopped
or redirected because of evidence, with the designer driving that call.
Weak signals: projects ending at handoff; "we" with no "I"; shipping volume as
the outcome.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Junior | Delivers assigned work reliably to handoff. |
| Mid | Owns features end to end, follows them through to launch. |
| Senior | Initiates work, drives it through ambiguity to shipped outcomes, follows up after launch, has stopped or redirected work on evidence. |
| Staff | Owns outcomes for a product area; identifies and drives work nobody asked for. |
| Principal | Owns long-horizon, cross-team bets; decides what the organisation should stop doing. |

## 10. Systems Thinking (Drive / Make)

Designing for consistency and scale. Design systems now serve machines as
well as people: tokens, component rules and documentation that AI generation
can consume. Also seeing how a change ripples across products, flows and
teams.

Look for: system contributions with reasoning behind structure, naming and
rules; documentation precise enough for others or AI tools to apply; thinking
beyond one feature (dependencies, reuse, downstream effects).
Weak signals: a component grid with no structure or rationale; "created a
design system" as the whole description; local solutions that ignore existing
patterns without saying why.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Junior | Uses the existing system correctly. |
| Mid | Extends the system sensibly; notices inconsistencies. |
| Senior | Contributes components or patterns with documented rationale; designs flows with downstream effects in mind. |
| Staff | Shapes system architecture (tokens, naming, governance) or cross-product patterns. |
| Principal | Defines how the system and patterns serve the whole company, including AI-generated output. |
