## 2. Assessment principles

All IC principles apply (evidence over adjectives, intent is not evidence,
separate the individual from the team). In addition:

- **The team's output is the leader's output, but only with a mechanism.**
  Credit a leader for team results when the portfolio shows what they
  changed that produced them (a hire, a process, a standard, a decision).
  Team results with no mechanism shown are context.
- **Before and after is the core evidence shape.** State of the team or
  practice on arrival, what the leader changed, state afterwards. Leadership
  case studies without a starting point are hard to assess.
- **Scope before seniority.** Read reporting lines and team size before
  reading titles. See levels.md.
- **IC case studies are not leadership evidence.** A leadership portfolio
  made mostly of the candidate's own design work reads as IC, whatever the
  title. Personal craft still counts for Quality Bar & Craft Leadership.
- **AI adoption is assessed through the practice.** How the leader changed
  the team's tools, workflows, quality bar and hiring for AI goes in
  Operating Model and Quality Bar. Leading AI product work goes in Strategy.
- **Confidentiality is normal.** Missing internal documents are not a red
  flag. Missing numbers and structure are a weakness.

## 3. Team Building & Org Design (People)

Hiring, team composition and structure: deciding what roles the organisation
needs, how teams map to product areas, setting a hiring bar, and building a
team that works without the leader in every decision.

Look for: hires made and how the bar was set (process, criteria, ladder);
team structure decisions and their reasoning (embedded vs central, ratios,
specialist roles); growth or reshaping of the team with numbers.
Weak signals: "built a world-class team" without structure or numbers; team
size quoted without reporting lines; hiring described only as volume.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Manager | Hires into own team against a clear bar; shapes roles within the team. |
| Senior Manager | Designs team structure across several teams in a domain; defines hiring process. |
| Director | Designs the org (managers or leads under them, team topology across domains); plans headcount with product and eng leadership. |
| Senior Director | Shapes the function's structure across the company, including specialist disciplines (research, content, design ops). |

## 4. People Development (People)

Growing people: coaching, feedback, performance management, career
frameworks, promotions, handling underperformance, retention.

Look for: specific coaching examples with an observable result (promotion,
scope increase, skill shift); career ladders or frameworks the leader built
or rebuilt; how underperformance was handled; engagement or retention data.
Weak signals: "mentored designers" with no example; development described as
values only; no sign of hard conversations.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Manager | Coaches ICs with specific results; runs performance cycles; handles underperformance. |
| Senior Manager | Develops leads or senior ICs; builds or applies a career framework across teams. |
| Director | Develops managers, or leads and staff ICs who carry coaching and delivery responsibility, with specific results; owns the ladder and promotion process for the org; succession thinking. |
| Senior Director | Develops directors; shapes talent strategy for the function. |

## 5. Quality Bar & Craft Leadership (Practice)

Setting and holding the standard of the work: critique, design reviews,
principles, and enough hands-on credibility to judge and improve work the
leader did not make.

Look for: principles, critique formats or review processes introduced, with
effect on output quality; before and after examples of the team's work; the
leader's own craft shown where relevant (prototypes, explorations, standards
demos); explicit statements of what "good" means and how it was enforced.
Weak signals: only the leader's personal portfolio pieces; quality claimed
with no examples of the team's work; principles listed with no sign they were
used.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Manager | Runs critique and reviews for the team; output quality visibly improved; can still do the work. |
| Senior Manager | Sets a consistent bar across several teams; principles or review formats adopted. |
| Director | Defines the quality bar for the org and its relation to product goals; still credible in the work. |
| Senior Director | Sets the company's design standard and how it is held across functions. |

## 6. Operating Model (Practice)

How design work gets done: process, rituals, design ops, tooling, design
system governance, AI-era workflows, and how design plugs into product
development.

Look for: rituals and processes introduced with reasons and effect (speed,
quality, predictability); system governance (ownership, contribution model,
adoption figures); tooling and AI workflow changes and what they changed;
lightweight process where heavy process was not needed.
Weak signals: process as a list of ceremonies; frameworks adopted with no
problem they solved; "introduced design ops" without detail.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Manager | Runs effective team rituals; integrates design into the team's delivery cycle. |
| Senior Manager | Standardises practice across teams; owns parts of system governance or tooling. |
| Director | Owns the design operating model for the org, including system governance and how AI tools are adopted. |
| Senior Director | Shapes how design, product and engineering work together company-wide. |

## 7. Design & Product Strategy (Direction)

Deciding where design should go and tying it to product and business
strategy: multi-quarter vision, priorities, design's role in product bets,
including AI product direction.

Look for: a strategy or vision with the problem it addressed, the choices
made and what was deprioritised; links to company goals; evidence the
strategy changed roadmaps or investment.
Weak signals: vision statements without choices; strategy decks shown as
artifacts with no effect; generic north stars.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Manager | Translates product strategy into a design plan for the team's area. |
| Senior Manager | Shapes design direction across a domain; influences domain roadmaps. |
| Director | Sets design strategy for a product line; co-shapes product strategy with product leadership. |
| Senior Director | Shapes company-level product and experience strategy. |

## 8. Cross-functional & Executive Partnership (Direction)

Working as a peer to product and engineering leadership and as a trusted
partner to executives: alignment, disagreement, decision rights, getting
design a seat where decisions are made.

Look for: specific decisions the leader changed with peers or executives;
how disagreements were resolved; reporting line and regular forums
(leadership team membership, exec reviews); design's role changing in the
company.
Weak signals: "partnered with stakeholders" without names of roles or
decisions; exec access claimed without examples; outcomes credited to the
leader that depended on others.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Manager | Strong partnership with PM and EM peers; influences team priorities. |
| Senior Manager | Partners with product and eng leads across a domain. |
| Director | Peer to product and engineering directors; regular exec exposure; changes cross-functional decisions. |
| Senior Director | Partner to C-level; part of company leadership forums. |

## 9. Business Impact (Outcomes)

Outcomes the organisation can measure that the leader's work contributed to:
revenue, conversion, retention, cost, speed, quality metrics, team metrics.

Look for: metrics with baselines and time frames; the mechanism linking
design leadership to the result; honest scoping of contribution; misses and
what was learned.
Weak signals: company metrics (funding, valuation, total growth) presented as
personal results; numbers without baselines; only design-process metrics.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Manager | Team's work tied to product metrics on their area. |
| Senior Manager | Domain-level outcomes with a clear design contribution. |
| Director | Product-line outcomes; design investment justified in business terms. |
| Senior Director | Company-level outcomes; design's value made legible to the business. |

## 10. Leading Change (Outcomes)

Changing an organisation under uncertainty: founding a function, scaling it,
turning it around, restructuring, navigating layoffs or pivots, shifting the
practice to AI-era ways of working.

Look for: the starting situation and what made it hard; the sequence of
changes and why that order; resistance and how it was handled; the state
afterwards and how durable it was.
Weak signals: change described only as initiatives launched; no account of
resistance or trade-offs; no sense of what was left behind.

| Level | At level looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Manager | Improves a team through a significant change (re-org, new process, new domain). |
| Senior Manager | Leads change across several teams. |
| Director | Leads org-level change: building, scaling or turning around a design org. |
| Senior Director | Leads function-wide or company-wide change. |
